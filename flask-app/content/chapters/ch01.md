## Linux: A Brief History
The Linux Kernel was first developed and released by Linus Torvalds in 1991 and is the most widely used operating system across the world. Chosen for its free and open-source architecture, the Linux Kernel is at the heart of what we know of today as "Linux". What we call the Linux Operating system is run on the Linux Kernel. What can the Linux Kernel be run on? Just about anything! From the largest supercomputers in the world, to the computers that we have in our homes, to the cellphones we keep in our pockets, and even to tiny micro-controllers. Just about any computer architecture can run the Linux Kernel if there is a compiler for the physical hardware. The Linux Kernel is built on a series of libraries and software packages called **GNU** (a recursive acronym that stands for *GNU is not Unix*) developed under the founder of the project Richard Stallman. He was frustrated by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) and XEROX and their refusal to get him code for a printer, citing ownership and trade secrets. While the issue of tempermental printers has not gotten any better, the common world of open-source software has. Today, billions of lines of code are being shared under what is called a General Public License (GPL); even this very class is being licensed under that model, so that information, experience and knowledge can be freely shared across the world.

Before the days of developed software by professional coders paid by massive software companies, software was widely shared for the purpose of having shared software. Many of the standards and practices that we take as common today hadn't been developed (we will circle back to those standards more in later chapters), and the rules of how computers would work and talk to each other had yet to be formulated. In 1969, an [anti-trust case involving IBM](https://www.justice.gov/atr/case-document/united-states-memorandum-1969-case) went to the Supreme Court, and though the case was eventually dropped, IBM decided that they would no longer package software freely. They would sell licenses. This led directly to the rise of the Free Software movement. Their foundational principles are:

<ol id="list">
<li>The freedom to run the program as you wish for any purpose.</li>
<li>The freedom to study how the program works, and change it so it does your computing as you wish. Access to the source code is a precondition for this.</li>
<li>The freedom to redistribute copies so you can help others.</li>
<li>The freedom to distribute copies of your modified versions to others. By doing this, you can give the whole community a chance to benefit from your changes. Access to the source code is a precondition for this.</li>
</ol>



## Supported Architecture
The list of supported architectures changes from time to time as technologies become out-moded. As of 2026 (version 7.0 of Linux Kernel) the list of specific architectures is too long and otherwise outside the scope of what we want to learn. But among the highlights are:
- Intel's x86 architecture - incredibly common in the consumer computing world. Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, and the other most common computer components run on this architecture.
- ARM - Used in the formats where power consumption is a factor,  like embedded systems for example. Embedded systems using ARM technology can vary in size, functionality, and features. They can be as small as smart-watches, or they can be used in laser-guided missiles. Your mileage may vary. 
- RISC (Reduced Instruction Set Computers) - Another architecture for small form-factor applications. The difference is that ARM architecture is proprietary and requires signficant royalties to use, while RISC-V is an open-source standard (more on that next).

## Operating System Licensing
For Windows and Apple users, the price of the operating system is often baked into the purchase of the consumer device. Could you imagine buying a MacBook then having to write the operating system on which it ran? What would you do if you bought your new gaming rig and found that the only instructions you could give the computer are in the BIOS? The operating system is licensed by the respective company that develops and supports the product. This is one business model, and its a perfectly fine way to operate. A user pays for an operating system that gets frequent updates from the manufacturer to fix security issues, technical bugs, and performance optimizations. "Linux" is different.

The Linux kernel is free under a Free and Open-Source licensing model. A licensing model is a legal framework under which a person can allow others intellectual property, while also reserving all the rights and privileges of it under the law. Copyright is the protection of creative works; copyright law keeps thousands of legal minds every year engaged in the process of protecting the rights of authors, artists, and other creative minds. Computer software is no different from the other creative works.

## Distros
If we were to do an Internet Search on Linux Operating System - we might find hundreds or thousands of different Linux operating system versions, called *distros*. A distro is a collection of applications that some person put together because they had need of a unique combination of applications, services, or default settings that they wanted to be standard on their device. Perhaps it was because they had a legacy application that they needed a specific library (more on libraries later). Or maybe it was because they wanted their distro to be focused on some task or issue. Whatever the reason, distros come in all shapes, sizes, feature-sets, footprints, languages. Some are serious, meant for serious tasks ([Mint](https://linuxmint.com/) and [Debian](https://www.debian.org/) come to mind). Some make a statement we can get behind ([RedStarOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Star_OS)). Some make a statement we shouldn't be anywhere near ([Uwu-ntu](https://uwuntuos.site/about-uwuntu/_gl-1/)). Still some are just outright jokes (looking at you, [Hannah Montana Linux](https://hannahmontana.sourceforge.net/)). The site [DistroWatch](distrowatch.com) tries to track and catalog distros and their popularity.
